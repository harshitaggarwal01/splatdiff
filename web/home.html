Three steps. The check takes about a minute.

  1. Film the safe setup

    A 20-second pan of the room, filmed after a discharge, a near-miss, or when a new walker arrives — the day it is set up the way the OT asked for. Say what must stay put: type it, or just speak it, and Second Look listens.

  2. Film again next visit

    The same slow pan from the doorway, lights on, bed-to-bathroom path included. The same corners matter more than the same angle — if we can’t see something, we say not seen, never missing.

  3. See what changed — and what to do

    High-risk items first. Every finding shows its evidence and ends with a plain “Do this next” step.

Every claim shows its receipts

A reasoning model writes the report, but it is not allowed the last word. Each claim it makes is held against SAM 3.1 detections — boxes, frame numbers and confidences from your two videos. If the evidence agrees, the claim stays and carries its receipts with it.

If the evidence does not agree, the claim is not quietly deleted. It is crossed out in front of you, with the reason.

Why it’s different

It compares visits, not just rooms. Inventory apps catalog a room; fall detectors act after a fall. Second Look tells you what changed between visits — and proves it.

Models check each other. Muse Spark proposes, SAM 3.1 must prove it, SAM’s image re-check can veto its own tracker, and Muse Wonder breaks ties — as in the verified walker and armchair moments above.

Dad has a say. His watchlist, in his words — “I moved the lamp to read” — makes that the new setup, and updates are written to him, not just about him.

Findings that lead to action

High-risk findings surface first, gathered in a “Needs attention” banner at the top of the report — a walker out of reach outranks a moved plant. Every high or medium finding ends in “Do this next”: plain steps, such as moving the walker back within arm’s reach of the bed and asking Dad to show you he can reach it.

Who should move it back? Only someone who is there, with Dad. If you are far away, send the report to someone local or the care team — never ask Dad to move furniture alone at night.

Done it? Mark it as sorted. The item stays on your watchlist and is checked again on the next visit; if it has moved again, it comes back. And the summary is easy to share — text, email, or print for the care folder — so siblings who were not there see the same evidence.

Moved on purpose? Dad and the family decide — one tap makes today the new setup.

Private by design

There is no installed camera and nothing recording between visits. Filming is deliberate, per visit — ideally with Dad, of rooms he is happy to share.

People and paper documents are blurred before any frame is stored, shown or sent to the reasoning model. SAM 3.1 itself sees the raw frames because it is the detector — the blurring, and everything after it, happens on what SAM 3.1 finds.

Raw videos are deleted as soon as the check finishes, unless someone chooses to keep them. Reports and frames that are kept are stored on the machine running Second Look (in its outputs/ folder), under your control — and any check can be deleted in the app.

Clips are sent to Meta’s model API only to find objects, using the standard model IDs (muse-spark-1.3, not muse-spark-1.3-contributor — see the code). There are no accounts, no ads and no analytics or ad scripts on this page or in the app.

On your own server, the Meta API key stays server-side. On this public demo, your key stays in your browser tab only and is sent only to api.meta.ai — never to us. The code is open source, so anyone can check all of this.

Not a medical device — it reports room changes, not health.

How Meta’s models work together

Four run every check; two built it.

muse-spark-1.3
Reasons. Takes the inventory of both rooms, suggests plainer names for hard-to-find objects, judges what moved, and writes the findings.
sam-3.1
Proves. Tracks every named object through both videos, then re-checks anything that seemed to appear or vanish. Its boxes are the evidence — or the reason a claim is struck.
muse-voice-transcribe-1.0
Listens. Turns a spoken care note (“the walker must stay by the bed”) into the watchlist, in about the time it takes to say it.
muse-image-1.0
Built the test room. Generated our scoreable sample room — and its edited twin, with a backpack removed, a lamp moved and a suitcase added — so the sample check has a known right answer.
muse-wonder-1.0
Second opinions. Breaks ties on borderline “moved” calls and reads past checks for patterns — it also wrote the code.

Honest numbers

On our one scoreable test room (3 runs), the raw model invented claims 25–40% of the time; grounding struck every one.

A smoke test, not a benchmark — real-room recall is barely tested — one real pair so far. We would rather show you one small true number than a large vague one. Questions families ask

Why a 20-second pan and not photos?

A short pan gives SAM 3.1 many frames of each object from slightly different angles, so a chair half-hidden in one still can still be tracked across the clip. It also matches what you already do on a visit — one slow look around the room — instead of asking you to photograph every corner.

Why not a camera in the room?

Because Dad lives there. An installed camera records between visits, whether or not anything is wrong; a phone pan happens on a visit, on purpose, ideally with him in the conversation. Deliberate filming also means consent is fresh every time, room by room.

Does it prevent falls?

No. Falls also come from strength, balance, medication and vision; a professional home-safety assessment stays the gold standard; and Second Look does not claim to reduce falls.

Does “seen where it was” mean it’s safe?

No — presence isn’t reachability. Second Look can report that the walker is still beside the bed; it cannot tell whether Dad can reach it from lying down, whether the wheels are locked, or whether the path to it is clear at 2 a.m. That judgement stays with you and his OT.

What does “not seen” mean?

It means we couldn’t confirm the item in this video — it may be out of frame, hidden behind furniture, or the light may have beaten us. It does not mean missing. Check in person before you worry.

What if it misses something?

That is the failure we worry about most: a struck false claim is harmless, but a silently missed “walker moved” is not. Recall on real, cluttered rooms is still untested, so treat Second Look as a supplement to your own look around — never a replacement for an OT home-safety check.

Is it a medical device?

No. Second Look reports room changes; it does not assess a person’s health, diagnose anything, or replace a professional safety assessment.
